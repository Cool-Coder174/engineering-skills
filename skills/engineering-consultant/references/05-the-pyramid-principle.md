# The Pyramid Principle

**Sources:** *The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving* —
Barbara Minto (Pearson, 3rd ed.), Ch. 1 "Why a Pyramid Structure?", Ch. 2 "The Substructures
Within the Pyramid", Ch. 3 "How to Build a Pyramid Structure". Supporting statements from
App. C "Summary of Key Points Mentioned in the Text". Content that the book predates carries
**Modern**.

This file holds the shape of every record this skill produces. A Comparison Record is a
pyramid. An Explanation is a pyramid. The verdict or the core idea sits at the top. The Key
Line sits under it. The evidence sits under the Key Line. Read this file before you write any
record, and again when a reviewer says a document is hard to follow.

**Minto writes about business memos. The reader of this file works on software.** Every
paragraph marked **In engineering** is a translation. It is not a report of what Minto wrote.

---

## 1. Why a pyramid

**The mind sorts information into pyramids without being asked.** Minto Ch. 1, opening, p. 1.
She states three findings. The mind sorts information into pyramidal groupings to comprehend
it. Any grouping is easier to comprehend when it arrives already sorted. Therefore the writer
must structure the document as a pyramid on purpose.

**Unclear writing is an ordering fault, not a sentence fault.** Minto, Introduction to Part
One, p. 1. She names style and order as the two causes, and calls order the more common cause
and the easier one to correct.

**The reader spends mental energy in three places.** Minto Ch. 1, p. 7. He recognizes the
words. He sees the relations between the ideas. He comprehends their significance. Only the
third place holds your message. **In engineering.** A comparison that describes each candidate,
then names the criteria, then states a verdict, spends that energy on assembly.

## 2. Sorting into pyramids and the magical number seven

**The mind groups any items it treats as having a common fate.** Minto Ch. 1, "Sorting into
Pyramids", p. 2. Her term is **common fate**. Six dots at uneven spacing read as two groups of
three. The reader will impose a pattern, and your only choice is whether the pattern is yours.

**The mind holds about seven items at one time.** Minto Ch. 1, "The Magical Number Seven",
p. 3. She credits the phrase to George A. Miller. Some minds hold nine items and some hold five.
She calls three a convenient number and one the easiest number. Above four or five items the
mind builds its own categories.

*Worked example — the shopping list (Ch. 1, pp. 3–4).* A husband walks toward the door to buy a
newspaper, and his wife names nine items one at a time as he crosses the room. Minto reports
that most men return with the newspaper and the grapes. She then prints the same nine items as a
list and asks the reader to name a supermarket section for each item. Most readers then
remember all nine.

**A grouping with no name gains you nothing.** Minto Ch. 1, "The Need to State the Logic", p. 4.
Nine items divided into sets of four, two, and three still come to nine. Naming the three sets
moves you to three, one level of abstraction higher. Minto adds that each person holds one giant
conglomeration of related pyramids. Communication makes what you say fit into one of them.

**In engineering.** A matrix with nine criteria will not be retained. Group the criteria into
three to five named groups before you score anything. The cap and the regrouping rule are in
`07-logical-order-and-mece.md`.

## 3. Two directions of work

Minto separates the direction of delivery from the direction of discovery. The two run
opposite ways, and this is the point that most writers miss.

| Direction | What it governs | The rule | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordering from the Top Down** | Delivery to the reader | State the summarizing idea before the ideas it summarizes | Ch. 1, p. 5 |
| **Thinking from the Bottom Up** | Discovery by the writer | Group the sentences, name the summary, repeat until no relation remains | Ch. 1, pp. 8–9 |

**Controlling the sequence is the most important act in clear writing.** Minto Ch. 1, pp. 5–6.
She adds that the reader looks for a connecting structure whether or not you supply one.

*Worked example — the Zurich beards (Ch. 1, pp. 5–6).* Minto tells a listener that she counted
fifteen bearded men in Zurich in fifteen minutes, then mentions moustaches in New York offices,
then mentions facial hair in London. The listener guesses in turn that Zurich is becoming
unconservative, that a comparison of cities is coming, and that the speaker is preoccupied with
beards. He concludes that London leads the other cities. Minto calls the conclusion perfectly
logical and wrong. Her point was how far facial hair has become accepted in business life.

**The major ideas are always derived from the minor ones.** Minto, Introduction to Part One.
You cannot derive the top from nothing.

**In engineering.** The derivation rule guards against a chosen favourite with reasons added
later. Build the criteria and the scores, read them from the bottom up, and ask what they
compel. If they would support a different verdict equally, you have no apex. See entry X-25.

## 4. The three rules a pyramid must obey

Minto states the rules at Ch. 1, "Thinking from the Bottom Up", p. 9, and explains them at
pp. 10–11. They are a test you can run before you write.

1. **Ideas at any level must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them.** A node that
   is not a summary of its children fails.
2. **Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea.** If the first idea is a
   reason for an action, every other idea must be a reason for the same action.
3. **Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered.** Minto Ch. 1, p. 10: "There must
   be a specific reason why the second idea comes second, and cannot come first or third."

**The plural-noun shortcut checks rule 2.** Minto Ch. 1, p. 10. You must be able to label the
grouping with one plural noun. *Reasons*, *steps*, *problems*, *changes*, *criteria*. Apples
and pears summarize as fruits. Apples and chairs summarize only as "things", which Minto calls
far too broad to indicate the logic of the grouping.

**The order must reflect the analysis that formed the group.** Minto Ch. 1, p. 11. She names
four analytical activities and four matching orders.

| The analysis that formed the group | The required order |
|---|---|
| You reasoned deductively | Argument order |
| You determined cause-and-effect relations | Time order |
| You commented on an existing structure | The order the structure dictates |
| You created a class | Order of importance |

**Run the rules as a gate before you write.** Minto Ch. 1, p. 11: "If any of the rules is
broken, it is an indication that there is a flaw in your thinking." She names three faults. The
thinking is flawed. The ideas are not developed. The ideas do not relate clearly.

**In engineering.** Rule 2 forbids a Key Line that mixes a criterion, a constraint, and a risk.
Throughput is a criterion. A pinned Rust dependency is a constraint. A vendor acquisition is a
risk. Three plural nouns means three groupings.

## 5. The vertical relationship — a question-and-answer dialogue

**Minto defines an idea as a statement that raises a question in the reader's mind, because you
tell him something he does not know.** Ch. 2, p. 14. A box that raises no question is not an idea.

The dialogue procedure, from Ch. 2, p. 14.

1. Put one idea in each box.
2. Let that statement raise its logical question. Why? How? Why do you say that?
3. Answer that question horizontally on the line below.
4. Accept that your answer raises further questions. Answer them on the next line below.
5. Continue until you judge that the reader holds no more logical questions.
6. Leave that leg. Return to the **Key Line** and answer the next question the top box raised.

**The timing rule.** Minto Ch. 2, p. 14. Do not raise a question before you are ready to answer
it. Do not answer a question before you have raised it. Agreement is not the standard. The
reader must follow the argument clearly.

*Worked example — Chesterton on pigs (Exhibit 2, Ch. 2, pp. 14–16).* G. K. Chesterton argues
that pigs should be kept as pets. Why? They are beautiful, and they could be bred to
fascinating variations. What makes a pig beautiful? It is marvelously fat, and it is typically
English. What is beautiful about being fat? It presents curves to the onlooker and it creates
modesty in the possessor. Minto chose an argument whose content most readers reject, to prove
that the structure works on its own.

**In engineering.** State the verdict. The reader asks why. The Key Line answers with the
criteria. Each criterion raises "on what evidence", and the cell under it answers with a
source, a version, and a date. A criterion with no cell is an unanswered question.

## 6. The horizontal relationship — deduction or induction

**Points on one line must present a clear argument, and only one kind of argument.** Minto
Ch. 2, "The Horizontal Relationship", p. 17: "They must present a clear inductive or deductive
argument, one or the other, but not both at once."

| Form | The shape of the group | How you summarize upward | Minto's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | A statement about the world, a comment on its subject or predicate, then the implication of both | Rest the summary heavily on the final point | Men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Ch. 2, p. 17 |
| **Inductive** | Like ideas that one plural noun describes | Draw an inference about what is the same | French, German, and Russian tanks at the Polish border. Ch. 2, p. 17 |

*Worked example — tanks at the Polish border (Ch. 2, p. 17).* Three statements name three
armies at one border. The plural noun that covers them is warlike movements against Poland. The
inference above them is that Poland is about to be invaded by tanks. That top point is a
judgement about the sameness, and not a deduction from any one member.

**Prefer induction on the Key Line.** Minto App. C, Ch. 5, p. 236, and Ch. 3, Caveat 6, p. 32.
Her stated reason is that induction takes the reader less effort. Both forms, the
discrimination test, and the two deductive exceptions are in `07-logical-order-and-mece.md`.

## 7. The top-down build procedure

**Signal to use it.** You know the subject. You can name the reader. You can state what he
already knows. Minto Ch. 3, opening, p. 21, calls this direction the easier of the two and
tells you to try it first.

The procedure, from Ch. 3, "The Top-Down Approach", pp. 22–23, with steps 7 and 8 completed in
her demonstration at pp. 25–26.

1. **Draw the top box.** Write the subject of the document in it.
2. **Decide the Question.** Visualize the reader. Name the question you want answered in his
   mind about the subject.
3. **Write the Answer**, or record that you can answer the question.
4. **Identify the Situation.** State the first noncontroversial thing you can say about the
   subject that the reader will accept as true.
5. **Develop the Complication.** Imagine the reader agrees and asks "so what". Name what
   happened in that Situation to raise the Question.
6. **Recheck the Question against the Complication.** The Complication must raise the Question
   you wrote in step 2. If it does not, change the Question, or change the Complication.
7. **Find the New Question and the Key Line.** Decide whether you answer deductively or
   inductively, and name the plural noun. Minto Ch. 3, p. 25: the answer to any Why? question
   is always reasons.
8. **Structure the support points.** Repeat the question-and-answer process one level down.

**The elements check each other.** Minto Ch. 3, Exhibit 4, p. 22. The Question checks the
Complication, the Complication checks the Question, and the Answer checks both.

*Worked example — Big Chief (Exhibits 5 and 6, Ch. 3, pp. 23–26).* A soft-drinks company bills
from delivery tickets on a five-week cycle. A large customer proposes to keep the tickets,
record them on a disk, and send the disk with a monthly check. The Accounting Department head
is asked whether the proposal is a good idea, and his memo describes record formats, extract
programs, and balancing procedures. Minto captions that exhibit "The points do not answer the
question". She runs the procedure instead. Subject is the requested change. Question is whether
it makes sense. Answer is yes. The New Question is why, so the Key Line holds three reasons. It
gives the company the information it needs. It increases cash flow. It reduces the work load.

**In engineering.** The Big Chief fault is the standard fault of a tool evaluation. Asked
whether to adopt a library, the writer explains how the library works. The mechanism is
accurate and it answers nothing. Run step 6 before you research anything.

## 8. The bottom-up build procedure

**Signal to use it.** Your thinking is not developed enough to build the top of the pyramid.
Minto Ch. 3, "The Bottom-Up Approach", p. 26, names three signs. You cannot fix the subject.
The Question is not clear. You cannot separate what the reader knows from what he does not.

The procedure, from Ch. 3, p. 26.

1. **List every point you think you want to make.**
2. **Determine the relations between them.**
3. **Draw conclusions.**

Three sub-rules from her demonstration at pp. 27–29.

- **Examine the recommendations first.** Minto states that the validity of an action idea is
  easier to determine than the validity of a situation idea.
- **Discard a grouping whose members say the same thing.** Two actions that restate each other
  are one action.
- **Draw the cause-and-effect relations as a picture.** The picture reveals separate lines of
  reasoning, and missing points.

Then think the introduction through. What the reader already knows decides which of the
competing conclusions you are actually making.

*Worked example — the TTW composing room (Exhibits 7 and 8, Ch. 3, pp. 27–30).* A young
consultant sends his manager a flat sequence of observations about an English printing company.
Low productivity. Uncompetitive prices. Behind schedule. High overtime. Two recommendations
follow at the end. Minto captions the original "The reasoning rambles". She examines the two
recommendations first and finds that both say the same thing. She then draws the problem points
as cause-and-effect chains, which exposes two separate lines of reasoning. The conclusions
become one Key Line of two actions. The two documents differ little in sentence craft.

**The 30-second rule.** Minto Ch. 3, p. 29. State the Key Line points at the top with the
introduction. If the reader cannot see your whole thinking in the first 30 seconds, rewrite.
The rule is treated in full in `06-the-scqa-introduction.md`.

### Which direction to start

| If… | Then… | Because |
|---|---|---|
| You can name the reader and his Question | Build top down | Ch. 3, p. 21 — the easier direction |
| You hold a pile of findings and no verdict | Build bottom up, then invert | Ch. 3, p. 26 |
| You want to write the whole document first | Neither. Stop | Ch. 3, Caveat 1, p. 31 |
| The verdict arrived before the evidence | Rebuild bottom up | Intro to Part One — the top is derived |

## 9. The six caveats for beginners

Minto states these at Ch. 3, "Caveats for Beginners", pp. 31–33. Each one carries the signal
that triggers it.

**Caveat 1. "Always try top down first."** Ch. 3, p. 31. *Signal:* you want to write the whole
document first and structure it later. Minto's reason is that a typed idea acquires an
extraordinary beauty, which makes you unwilling to revise it.

**Caveat 2. Start your thinking from the Situation.** Ch. 3, p. 31. *Signal:* you are about to
write the introduction. You may place the four elements in any order, because the order changes
only the tone. Always think from the Situation.

**Caveat 3. Do not omit the introduction.** Ch. 3, p. 31. *Signal:* the main point feels obvious,
so you want to move straight to the Key Line. You then structure material that belongs in the
Situation, and you force yourself into an unwieldy deductive argument.

**Caveat 4. Put historical chronology in the introduction.** Ch. 3, pp. 31–32. *Signal:* you
want to tell the reader what happened. Minto Ch. 3, p. 32: "Simple historical occurrences do not
exist as the result of logical thought, and therefore cannot be included as ideas." Events
appear in the body only inside a cause-and-effect relation.

**Caveat 5. Limit the introduction to what the reader will agree is true.** Ch. 3, p. 32: "The
introduction is meant to tell the reader only what he already knows." The rule runs both ways.
Material the reader does not know distorts his Question. Material he does know, used inside the
pyramid, implies that the introduction is incomplete.

**Caveat 6. Given a choice, use induction on the Key Line.** Ch. 3, pp. 32–33. *Signal:* you
are about to present the argument in the order you discovered it, which is usually deductive.
Minto states that induction at the Key Line takes the reader less effort. That you developed
the ideas deductively does not oblige you to present them that way.

**In engineering.** Caveat 5 has a repository form. **Modern.** The pinned versions, the deploy
target, and the migration in progress belong in the Situation, because the reader can check
them. A benchmark you ran last week does not.

## 10. The Pacific Avenue warehouse — a defensible recommendation

*Worked example — Ch. 3, Caveat 6, pp. 32–33.* Minto supports a recommendation to buy a
warehouse with a deductive Key Line. She then observes that the third point of that chain
raises no question, and that the reader does not need it. Minto Ch. 3, p. 33: "This is an
overstructured argument, and signals that the inductive form would more efficiently communicate
your message." She restates the recommendation inductively. The top point becomes "Buy the
Pacific Avenue warehouse because it meets our criteria". The three Key Line points become the
criteria. It is on a corner. It is larger than 5,000 square feet. It is under $10 a square
foot.

**Three properties make that recommendation defensible.**

1. The verdict names the option and the reason in one sentence.
2. The reason is that the option meets stated criteria. It is not that other options failed.
3. Each criterion carries a threshold that a reader can check against a real building.

**A dissenter can point at one box.** He can reject the 5,000 square foot threshold, or dispute
the corner requirement, or argue that $10 is the wrong price. He never has to accept or reject
the whole recommendation as one lump.

**In engineering.** "Adopt Postgres because it meets our criteria" sits above three criteria
with thresholds. It sustains p99 under 50 ms at 10,000 writes per second. Its licence permits a
distributed binary. It publishes a supported export path. The thresholds come from the R2 table
in `08-defining-and-structuring-the-problem.md`.

## 11. What "defensible" means in this skill

**Minto states the standard at Ch. 2, "The Vertical Relationship", p. 16.** She describes a
20-page memorandum that recommends the purchase of a British Leyland franchise. It gives three
reasons, and under each reason sits the answer to the next question the reader would raise. The
reasoning is stated so clearly that "the reader is in a position to determine whether he
disagrees with the writer's reasoning, and to raise logical questions concerning it".

**That sentence is this skill's definition of a defensible recommendation.** It sets two
requirements, and neither one is agreement.

| Requirement | What it obliges the writer to do | Fault when it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| The reader can determine whether he disagrees | Every claim sits in a named box at a named level | The reader must accept or reject the whole document |
| The reader can raise logical questions about it | Each box answers the question the box above raises | The reader has no place to attach his objection |

**A recommendation is not defensible because it is correct.** It is defensible because a
dissenter can name the exact box he rejects. The Chesterton argument proves the point. Its
content is absurd and its structure is sound.

**In engineering.** **Modern.** Three habits make an engineering record defensible. Put the
threshold in the header row, so a cell can be wrong. Cite a source, a version, and a date on
every filled cell, so a cell can be rechecked. State the reversal condition.

## 12. Pyramid faults and their repair

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Repair | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The reader reaches a conclusion you did not intend | The framework arrived after the instances | State the summarizing idea first | Ch. 1, pp. 5–6 |
| A grouping of nine items, or one summarized as "considerations" | The magical number seven, or unlike ideas in one group | Group into three to five, and split by plural noun | Ch. 1, pp. 3, 10 |
| No reason exists for the order of the points | Rule 3 is broken | Name the analysis that formed the group, then use its order | Ch. 1, pp. 10–11 |
| A box that raises no question | It is not an idea | Delete it, or promote what it supports | Ch. 2, p. 14 |
| One line mixes a chain and a list | Deduction and induction at once | Choose one form | Ch. 2, p. 17 |
| The document explains how a thing works, and was asked whether to do it | The points do not answer the Question | Rerun the top-down procedure, step 6 | Ch. 3, pp. 23–26 |
| Findings listed as collected, with a conclusion at the end | Order of discovery, not order of argument | Run the bottom-up procedure, then invert | Ch. 3, pp. 27–29 |
| The last Key Line point raises no question | An overstructured argument | Restate the Key Line inductively | Ch. 3, p. 33 |
| The reader cannot tell which point to argue with | No pyramid, so no defensibility | Rebuild bottom up, then present top down | Ch. 2, p. 16 |

## 13. When this file does not apply

**A single fact is not a pyramid.** One cited answer to one narrow question has no Key Line and
no grouping. A two-sentence reply needs no build procedure. The rules govern the record, not
the conversation.

**"Not applicable" is a valid result.** It is better than an invented structure.

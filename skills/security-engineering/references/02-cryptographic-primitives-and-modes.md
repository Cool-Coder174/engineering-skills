# Cryptographic Primitives and Modes of Operation

**Source:** Stallings, *Cryptography and Network Security*, 4th ed., Chapters 2, 3, 5, 6.

Most crypto findings in application code are not broken algorithms. They are a correct algorithm
used in a mode that does not provide the property the code assumes it provides. This file is about
that gap.

---

## 1. What the attacker is assumed to know (§2.2, p. 33)

Table 2.1 (p. 33) enumerates attack models by what the cryptanalyst has:

| Model | Cryptanalyst has |
|---|---|
| Ciphertext only | Algorithm, ciphertext |
| Known plaintext | Algorithm, ciphertext, one or more plaintext–ciphertext pairs under the key |
| Chosen plaintext | Algorithm, ciphertext, plaintexts of the attacker's choosing plus their ciphertexts |
| Chosen ciphertext | Algorithm, ciphertext, purported ciphertexts of the attacker's choosing plus their decryptions |
| Chosen text | Both of the above |

Two consequences for review.

**The algorithm is not a secret.** "In some cases, not even the encryption algorithm is known, but
in general we can assume that the opponent does know the algorithm used for encryption" (p. 33).
Any argument that rests on the attacker not knowing your scheme is not a security argument. And:
"Only relatively weak algorithms fail to withstand a ciphertext-only attack. Generally, an
encryption algorithm is designed to withstand a known-plaintext attack" (p. 34). A real primitive
is expected to survive an attacker who has matched pairs — so your custom scheme has to as well.

**Known plaintext is the normal case, not the exotic one.** Stallings' examples are structural:
"a file that is encoded in the Postscript format always begins with the same pattern, or there may
be a standardized header or banner to an electronic funds transfer message" (p. 33). Your
equivalents: JSON always starts with `{"`, protobuf field tags, a fixed envelope header, an
enum with three possible values. This is why ECB leaks and why "the attacker doesn't know the
plaintext" is rarely true.

Where the code decrypts attacker-supplied ciphertext and the response differs on padding failure
versus MAC failure versus success, you have handed over a **chosen-ciphertext** oracle. Note also
the definition's demand that the attack succeed *without* the key: "If either type of attack
succeeds in deducing the key, the effect is catastrophic: **All future and past messages encrypted
with that key are compromised**" (p. 33). That sentence is the argument for key separation and
rotation in `03-randomness-and-key-management.md`.

## 2. What "secure" means (§2.2, pp. 34–35)

> "An encryption scheme is **unconditionally secure** if the ciphertext generated by the scheme
> does not contain enough information to determine uniquely the corresponding plaintext, no matter
> how much ciphertext is available… With the exception of a scheme known as the one-time pad …
> there is no encryption algorithm that is unconditionally secure." (p. 34)

Everything real is **computationally secure**, which Stallings defines by two criteria (p. 34):

- "The cost of breaking the cipher exceeds the value of the encrypted information."
- "The time required to break the cipher exceeds the useful lifetime of the information."

Then the caveat: "The rub is that it is very difficult to estimate the amount of effort required to
cryptanalyze ciphertext successfully" (p. 34).

**How to use this in review.** These two criteria are the only legitimate way to argue that a
weaker mechanism is acceptable, and both are about *your data*, not about the algorithm. A
short-lived session token has a genuinely short useful lifetime. A password hash, a customer
record, and a health record do not — they must resist an attacker who steals the ciphertext today
and cracks it in five years. So "who cares, it's just internal" is not one of the criteria, and
"the data expires in 30 days" only counts if the *sensitivity* expires in 30 days too.

Table 2.2, "Average Time Required for Exhaustive Key Search" (p. 35), is where DES dies: "at this
performance level, DES can no longer be considered computationally secure" (p. 35). Note the "on
average, half of all possible keys must be tried" (p. 33) — a 56-bit key means 2⁵⁵ expected work,
not 2⁵⁶.

### 2.1 The one-time pad, and why it isn't the answer (§2.5, p. 48)

Worth reading because it isolates exactly which property does the work:

> "The security of the one-time pad is entirely due to the randomness of the key. If the stream of
> characters that constitute the key is truly random, then the stream of characters that constitute
> the ciphertext will be truly random." (p. 48)

And the two reasons you cannot use it: "the practical problem of making large quantities of random
keys," and "Even more daunting is the problem of key distribution and protection. For every
message to be sent, a key of equal length is needed by both sender and receiver" (p. 48).

**The trap to watch for:** an engineer who has read half of this implements "XOR with a secret
string" and calls it a one-time pad. It is a repeating-key Vigenère cipher, breakable by hand.
The pad must be *random*, *as long as the message*, and *used once* — the last two are what
homemade versions always violate.

## 3. Key length

Stallings' floor, stated for stream ciphers but explicitly generalized — "The same considerations
as apply for block ciphers are valid here" — is:

> "with current technology, a key length of at least **128 bits** is desirable." (§6.3, p. 190)

Historical anchors for reading old code: DES is 56 bits and dead (p. 35, p. 82). Two-key 3DES
gives 112 bits and drew enough concern that "many researchers now feel that three-key 3DES is the
preferred alternative" (§6.1, p. 179). AES's minimum is 128 (p. 35).

> **Modern.** 128-bit symmetric keys remain the floor; AES-128 and AES-256 are both fine, and
> 256-bit is standard where post-quantum margin is wanted. Public-key sizes have moved: see
> `04-public-key-and-key-exchange.md`. 3DES is deprecated (its 64-bit block, not its key, is now
> the binding limit) and RC4 is prohibited in TLS. Do not read §6 as endorsing them today; read it
> for the reasoning.

## 4. Block vs stream, and the one rule you cannot break

A stream cipher XORs plaintext with a keystream generated from the key: "a pseudorandom stream is
one that is unpredictable without knowledge of the input key" (§6.3, p. 189). Structurally it is a
one-time pad with a pseudorandom pad instead of a random one (p. 190).

Design considerations (p. 190): large period, keystream statistically indistinguishable from
random, and sufficient key length.

Then the critical operational difference:

> "The advantage of a block cipher is that you can reuse keys. However, **if two plaintexts are
> encrypted with the same key using a stream cipher, then cryptanalysis is often quite simple**.
> If the two ciphertext streams are XORed together, the result is the XOR of the original
> plaintexts. If the plaintexts are text strings, credit card numbers, or other byte streams with
> known properties, then cryptanalysis may be successful." (§6.3, p. 190)

**This is the single most portable crypto rule for code review.** Any keystream-based construction
— a stream cipher, or a block cipher in CTR, OFB, or CFB mode, or AES-GCM — is fatally broken by
reusing the (key, nonce) pair. Two messages encrypted with the same keystream leak their XOR, and
with AEAD constructions nonce reuse can additionally leak the authentication key. So:

- A hardcoded, zeroed, or constant IV/nonce with a counter-style mode is not a hygiene issue. It
  is a plaintext-recovery bug (V-02, V-04).
- A random 96-bit GCM nonce is safe for a very large but finite number of messages under one key;
  a random 64-bit nonce is not. Counting matters.
- Deterministic encryption for searchability is a deliberate trade, and it leaks equality. It must
  be named as such, not arrived at accidentally.

RC4's history is also the best short lesson in protocol-versus-primitive blame: the WEP break was
"not with RC4 itself but the way in which keys are generated for use as input to RC4… This problem
points out the difficulty in designing a secure system that involves both cryptographic functions
and protocols that make use of them" (§6.3, p. 193).

## 5. Modes of operation (§6.2, pp. 181–189)

Table 6.1 (p. 181) gives Stallings' intended application per mode. The review-relevant reading:

| Mode | Construction | Stallings' typical application | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ECB** | Each block encrypted independently under the same key | "Secure transmission of single values (e.g., an encryption key)" (p. 181) | Anything longer than one block; repeated blocks are visible |
| **CBC** | Input is plaintext XOR previous ciphertext; IV for the first block | "General-purpose block-oriented transmission; authentication" (p. 181) | Predictable or unauthenticated IV; no integrity check |
| **CFB** | Ciphertext fed back into a shift register; makes a stream cipher | "General-purpose stream-oriented transmission; authentication" (p. 181) | Keystream reuse |
| **OFB** | Cipher output fed back; makes a stream cipher | "Stream-oriented transmission over noisy channel" (p. 181) | Keystream reuse; trivially malleable |
| **CTR** | Plaintext XOR encrypted counter, no chaining | "General-purpose block-oriented; useful for high-speed requirements" (p. 181) | Counter reuse |

### 5.1 ECB (p. 181)

> "The most significant characteristic of ECB is that the same b-bit block of plaintext, if it
> appears more than once in the message, always produces the same ciphertext." (p. 182)

> "For lengthy messages, the ECB mode may not be secure. If the message is highly structured, it
> may be possible for a cryptanalyst to exploit these regularities. For example, if it is known
> that the message always starts out with certain predefined fields, then the cryptanalyst may
> have a number of known plaintext-ciphertext pairs to work with. If the message has repetitive
> elements, with a period of repetition a multiple of b bits, then these elements can be
> identified by the analyst. This may help in the analysis or may provide an opportunity for
> **substituting or rearranging blocks**." (p. 182)

Note that last clause: ECB is not only a confidentiality failure. Because blocks are independent,
an attacker can cut, splice, duplicate, and reorder them and the result still decrypts. ECB over a
serialized record is an authorization bug waiting to happen.

Stallings' sanctioned use — "ideal for a short amount of data, such as an encryption key" (p. 182)
— is why you occasionally see ECB legitimately in key-wrapping code. Modern practice replaces even
that with a dedicated key-wrap construction (AES-KW) or an AEAD.

### 5.2 CBC and the IV (pp. 183–184)

CBC exists to fix ECB: "we would like a technique in which the same plaintext block, if repeated,
produces different ciphertext blocks" (p. 183).

The IV requirements are stated precisely, and they are two distinct requirements:

> "The IV must be known to both the sender and receiver but **be unpredictable by a third party**.
> For maximum security, the IV should be **protected against unauthorized changes**." (p. 184)

Why unauthorized changes matter, in Stallings' own derivation: since `P₁ = IV ⊕ D(K, C₁)`,
complementing bit *i* of the IV complements bit *i* of the recovered first plaintext block. "This
means that if an opponent can predictably change bits in IV, the corresponding bits of the received
value of P₁ can be changed" (p. 184).

So an IV is public but must be **unpredictable** (rules out a counter or a timestamp for CBC) and
**integrity-protected** (rules out shipping it alongside unauthenticated ciphertext). A random IV
per message, covered by a MAC over IV‖ciphertext, satisfies both. In review, `iv = new byte[16]`
is a finding, and so is a randomly generated IV with no MAC.

### 5.3 CFB and OFB (pp. 184–187)

Both convert a block cipher into a stream cipher, which "eliminates the need to pad a message to
be an integral number of blocks" and "can operate in real time" (p. 184).

The trade between them is instructive because it is about *error behavior*, which is also
*attacker leverage*:

> "One advantage of the OFB method is that bit errors in transmission do not propagate…
> The disadvantage of OFB is that it is more vulnerable to a message stream modification attack
> than is CFB. Consider that complementing a bit in the ciphertext complements the corresponding
> bit in the recovered plaintext. Thus, controlled changes to the recovered plaintext can be made.
> This may make it possible for an opponent, by making the necessary changes to the checksum
> portion of the message as well as to the data portion, to alter the ciphertext in such a way
> that it is not detected by an error-correcting code." (p. 186)

Generalize it: **in any XOR-keystream mode, the attacker has bit-precise control over the
plaintext without knowing the key.** A CRC or checksum inside the ciphertext does not stop this,
because the attacker patches the checksum too. Only a real MAC does. This is the concrete
mechanism behind V-15 (encryption used as an integrity mechanism) and V-16 (integrity check
applied outside the encryption).

### 5.4 CTR (pp. 187–189)

> "The only requirement stated in SP 800-38A is that the **counter value must be different for
> each plaintext block that is encrypted**." (p. 187)

Its advantages (from [LIPM00], p. 188): hardware and software efficiency through parallelism,
preprocessing of the keystream, **random access** ("the ith block of plaintext or ciphertext can be
processed in random-access fashion"), provable security ("CTR is at least as secure as the other
modes discussed in this section"), and simplicity — "CTR mode requires only the implementation of
the encryption algorithm and not the decryption algorithm."

Random access is the property that makes CTR the right choice for encrypted blob storage with range
reads; it is also the property that makes reusing a counter across two objects catastrophic.

## 6. Do not compose your own primitives (§6.1, pp. 175–179)

Double DES looks like it should give 112 bits of security. It gives about 56.

> "The algorithm, known as a **meet-in-the-middle attack**… is based on the observation that, if we
> have C = E(K2, E(K1, P))… the result is that a known plaintext attack will succeed against double
> DES, which has a key size of 112 bits, with an effort on the order of 2⁵⁶, not much more than the
> 2⁵⁵ required for single DES." (pp. 176–177)

And crucially: it "does not depend on any particular property of DES but … will work against any
block encryption cipher" (p. 176).

**The general lesson for review.** Encrypting twice, encrypting-then-hashing, hashing twice,
truncating a tag, or XORing two constructions together are not intuitive security improvements.
They are new cryptosystems with unanalyzed properties, and the intuitive strength estimate is
routinely wrong by half. Any composition not taken from a standard is a finding (V-08).

The same chapter shows the correct posture when a weakness is found: 3DES was adopted precisely
because it "would preserve the existing investment in software and equipment" (p. 175) while
restoring margin — a standardized upgrade path, not a clever local patch.

## 7. Side channels (§3.4, p. 83)

> "In essence, a timing attack is one in which information about the key or the plaintext is
> obtained by observing how long it takes a given implementation to perform decryptions on various
> ciphertexts." (p. 83)

Stallings raises it for symmetric ciphers and develops it for RSA in Chapter 9. In application
code, the practical form is comparison, not decryption: a byte-by-byte `==` on a MAC, token, or
password hash returns early on the first mismatch and leaks the correct prefix. Use the platform's
constant-time comparison. This is V-21.

## 8. Why the primitives look the way they do (§3.1, §3.5)

Two ideas worth carrying, both arguments against homemade ciphers and homemade hashes.

**Avalanche.** "A small change in either the plaintext or the key should produce a significant
change in the ciphertext… a change in one bit of the plaintext or one bit of the key should produce
a change in many bits of the ciphertext. If the change were small, this might provide a way to
reduce the size of the plaintext or key space to be searched" (§3.3, pp. 80–81). The strict
avalanche criterion tightens this: "any output bit j of an S-box should change with probability 1/2
when any single input bit i is inverted for all i, j" (§3.5, p. 88).

**Rounds.** "The greater the number of rounds, the more difficult it is to perform cryptanalysis,
even for a relatively weak F. In general, the criterion should be that the number of rounds is
chosen so that known cryptanalytic efforts require greater effort than a simple brute-force key
search attack" (§3.5, p. 87).

Both are properties nobody achieves by accident, and neither is testable by "the output looks
scrambled." That is the whole case for using a vetted implementation of a vetted algorithm.

## 9. Decision guidance

Stallings gives you the mechanisms; here is how to apply them to a 2020s codebase.

| Need | Use | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Encrypt a message or record | An AEAD: AES-GCM, AES-GCM-SIV, or ChaCha20-Poly1305 | ECB anything; unauthenticated CBC/CTR |
| Encrypt a large stream | AEAD in chunks, with chunk index and total count bound into the AAD | A single unauthenticated pass |
| Encrypt with high message volume under one key | AES-GCM-SIV, or a counter-derived nonce with a documented budget | Random 64-bit nonces |
| Wrap a key | AES-KW, or an AEAD | Raw ECB |
| Integrity only, shared key | HMAC | A CRC, a bare hash, or a homemade keyed hash |
| Encrypt for a public key holder | Library hybrid encryption (KEM + AEAD) | Raw textbook RSA |

Non-negotiables to check in any diff that touches encryption:

- [ ] Is the mode authenticated? If not, where is the MAC, and does it cover the IV/nonce, the
      ciphertext, *and* the associated context (key id, version, record id)?
- [ ] Can the (key, nonce) pair ever repeat — across retries, replicas, restarts, or a restored
      backup? What guarantees it cannot?
- [ ] Is the IV/nonce generated per message from a CSPRNG (or a guaranteed-unique counter for CTR)?
- [ ] Is the decrypt path's error behavior uniform, so it is not a chosen-ciphertext oracle?
- [ ] Are all comparisons of secrets, tags, and tokens constant-time?
- [ ] Is the key length ≥ 128 bits, from a real KDF or KMS rather than a passphrase or a config
      string?
- [ ] Is there a version or algorithm identifier in the ciphertext envelope, so the scheme can be
      migrated later?
- [ ] Does anything in the design rely on the attacker not knowing the construction?
